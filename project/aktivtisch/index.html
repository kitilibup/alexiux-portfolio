Aktivtisch

2026
01

Product design for touch, made accessible for older adults.

‍

Aktivtisch is an interactive touchscreen table designed for care homes and elderly care settings. I ran the research, redesigned the main app, game apps, and created the design system with documentation.

Overview

‍

Role: Product Designer

Period: Aug 2025 – present

Team: Stakeholders · Unity developers · content designer · QA

Key skills: Mixed-methods research, Prioritisation, Accessibility for older adults and tremor, Touch and remote interaction design, Design systems, Prototyping and on-device testing, Design QA, Art direction

‍

Objectives

  • Learn why caregivers avoided settings.
  • Make settings, profiles and shutdown safe for untrained staff.
  • Bring nine game apps to one accessible standard.
  • Give the developers specs that explain themselves.

‍

Goal

  • Sales. Build an accessible, research-backed interface that gives care homes and investors a reason to buy.
  • Standing apart. Profiles set Aktivtisch apart from competing tables. Make them easy enough for daily use.
  • Consistency and scale. Build every screen and new app from one design system, so releases cost less.
  • Daily operation. Cut the time staff spend on setup, troubleshooting and navigation.
  • Engagement. Make sessions longer and more enjoyable with clearer type and calmer backgrounds.

‍

Context

‍

Three table sizes and a tablet

‍

The table comes in 43, 49 and 55 inch. Each one tilts into a table, lectern or board position. Aktivtisch GO runs the same apps on a tablet. Every layout I designed had to work on all of them.

‍

‍

‍

‍

‍

Cross-platform app for Windows and Android

‍

Residents watch from up to three metres away, so I kept layouts simple and legible. The table takes touch and a remote cursor. The 43-inch table runs Full HD; the 49 and 55 inch tables run 4K.

‍

I designed and specified a full library of accessible custom components, down to the keyboard. Each has a 64 × 64 px minimum touch target and Atkinson Hyperlegible type.

‍

‍

‍

‍

‍

Discover and define

‍

Benchmarking Aktivtisch against four competitors

‍

Buyers compare activity tables, so I benchmarked four competitors on format, training, support and features. Aktivtisch was the only table in three sizes and one of two with profiles.

‍

‍

‍

Caregivers run the table

‍

Research showed staff do more than 95% of the navigation. Residents tap the games. So I designed for two users at once.

‍

The table serves residents, caregivers who run sessions, caregivers who manage the table, and facility management. Personas are an old-school tool, but my proto-personas aligned stakeholders on these four groups. With my journey maps, they surfaced the pain points we prioritised. One was "I have to hold his hand just to lower the sound."

‍

‍

‍

Three problems shaped the redesign

‍

Settings nobody trusted. I designed a survey for our client facilities. 29 of the 308 invited answered. All found launching an activity easy, yet 55% felt unsure in settings. 59% rarely or never created profiles. Their comments named problems in the most-used features, like saving changes and adding web pages.

‍

Games that punished mistakes. In a heuristic evaluation of ten apps, I coded 105 notes against an 18-question checklist. In three apps, players got no second try after a wrong answer.

‍

A screen too big to reach. On two field visits in disability care, residents could not reach the top corners. They expected a tap to work the moment their finger landed.

‍

The root cause was a mismatch. The system thought in profiles and categories; caregivers thought "open an app, run the activity, done".

Prioritise

‍

Ranking 123 ideas before building any

‍

I ran a MoSCoW workshop with stakeholders and the developers. Nothing started as a Must-have. 38% to 48% of each list went to Won't have for the beta version. Stakeholders then adopted the ranking as the team's way to prioritise.

‍

Each idea also gets a development level from 1 to 3. Level 1 is daily use and gets the most care; level 3 only has to work.

‍

‍

‍

‍

‍

Design

‍

Touch targets for tremor, built from scratch

‍

I found no open-source design system covering the accessibility needs of older adults, particularly people living with dementia, tremors, or age-related vision problems. So I started with extensive research, reviewing studies and articles to validate my design decisions before moving into design. 

‍

I based my design decisions on research findings, translating identified accessibility needs into practical interface guidelines and reusable components.

‍

‍

‍

The remote moves a cursor across the screen, like a mouse. So I designed for touch and pointer at once: besides default and pressed, every control has a hover state that shows what the cursor is on.

‍

‍

Settings on one screen, in three groups

‍

All settings are visible at a glance, without the need for scrolling. Although the icons may lack visual consistency, their distinct appearances make them easier to recognise and differentiate. Prioritising recognisability over stylistic uniformity helps reduce cognitive effort and supports accessibility.

‍

‍

‍

‍

Wizards for complex flows

‍

I turned complex flows into a short wizard. For example, creating an album involves several potential points of failure: an empty name, a missing PIN, or a keyboard being closed halfway through. I specified every error state in Figma, including exactly when the red outline and tooltip should appear and disappear, so developers didn't have to guess how to implement them.

‍

‍

‍

‍

‍

‍

‍

Shut down

‍

Caregivers unplugged the table because shutdown was tucked away in the settings and needed a long press. I made shutting down easy to find and turned it into a proper PC shutdown. If someone unplugs the table anyway, the next start shows how to shut down safely.

‍

‍

‍

Profiles in one short form

‍

Facilities named profiles after sessions twice as often as after residents. So I made the form's example name a session, "Afternoon group".

‍

‍

‍

‍

‍

Consent and product keys

‍

German law requires Decline to be as easy as Allow, so I gave both equal weight. I renamed licence keys to product keys. Their errors show under the field.

‍

‍

‍

‍

A keyboard built for this screen

‍

Typing is the slowest input on the table. I designed a custom keyboard with German, English, Chinese and Hong Kong English layouts.

‍

‍

‍

‍

‍

Six rules every game must pass

‍

In Test Your Knowledge, Guess the Saying and Black Sheep apps, a wrong answer was final. From the review, I wrote six rules for every game. Each game keeps its mechanic, and every round ends. No wrong answer is final, and difficulty lives in the content. 

‍

‍

‍

‍

Consistent backgrounds with a human touch

‍

The review found busy backgrounds that hid the picture, and gloomy dark ones. Every redesigned game now sits on one family of light backgrounds in the brand greens. Hand-made illustrations stay sharp on every table size. Each game gets a small motif at the edges, so the middle stays clear.

‍

‍

‍

‍

Game app redesign example: Black Sheep

‍

Caregivers did not know the three apps were levels of one game. I merged them into one tile, entered by topic, with levels inside. I set difficulty by how much of the picture changes. A soap dish at 18% is Beginner; an umbrella at 0.54% is Expert. I audited 83 library photos against that spec and rejected 45.

‍

‍

‍

‍

Saving a painting with one field

‍

I removed the author field from the save dialog. With no name typed, the table names the painting after the profile, like "Anna's painting".

‍

‍

‍

‍

One set of media controls for songs, stories and videos

‍

Singing, the fairy tales and Advertising Classics each placed their buttons differently. Now they share the same buttons, sizes and states. Lyrics follow the song and can be hidden to show the whole picture.

‍

‍

‍

‍

‍

Testing

‍

‍

I coded prototypes for nine apps and made more than 25 offline prototypes. The builds also needed true full screen and an exit button for touch. 

‍

‍

‍

‍

‍

Deliver

‍

A design system the developers can build from

‍

In Figma, I built the Master File with about 1,600 components and variants. In Confluence, I wrote the UI guide on how the interface behaves and why. Every screen uses a 16-column grid with a 24 px safe area. The developers build each new app from tested components. The aim is a consistent product that scales, with less mess and cheaper releases.

‍

‍

‍

‍

‍

Localisation in 3 languages

‍

The table ships in German, English and Chinese. German runs about 40% longer than English, so I gave every text slot a length limit. I wrote notes with a character counter for the content designer. All imported German questions fit their slots.

‍

‍

‍

AI for prototypes, then design by hand

‍

I used AI only to build coded prototypes fast. Each one showed the developers how a screen should behave. Then I revised every design by hand in Figma.

‍

Animation. I designed every animation to a few timing rules. Screens cross-fade in 350 to 450 ms, and rewards get more time. The Black Sheep star bursts over the odd picture and is gone within 1.8 seconds.

‍

‍

‍

Brand

‍

One brand and customer experience

‍

I treated the brand as the whole customer experience. A care home meets Aktivtisch online and in print, then uses it every day. I art-directed the brand book and the January 2026 photo shoot with real residents. I also designed the new website concept. The logo set and flyers were made with an external designer. I chose Commissioner and Source Sans 3 for the brand, and Atkinson Hyperlegible inside the product.

‍

Brand consistency. Every touchpoint uses the same greens, the same two typefaces and photos of real residents. The website, flyers and social posts all follow the brand book, so a care home sees the same Aktivtisch in a brochure, online and on the table.

‍

App consistency. Inside the product, every app is built from the Master File: the same controls, sizes and states, one family of light backgrounds in the brand greens, and Atkinson Hyperlegible throughout. A caregiver who learns one app already knows where Back, Home and sound are in all the others.

‍

‍

Measure

‍

Measuring what keeps care homes using the table

‍

Content that never changes wears residents out. I proposed smaller, more frequent updates of about 1 GB each, and stakeholders made them monthly. Each update is a retention hook, and NEW badges on Home point caregivers to the fresh content.

‍

Regulations make care homes hard to visit, so every table reports its daily use. Counting opens of 15 seconds or more filters out accidental taps. Tables that stay offline store each day and send it later. I designed the consent with Decline as prominent as Allow.

What I learned

‍

Turn high-level ideas into testable hypotheses.

I translated high-level ideas into hypotheses, defining the user problem, proposed solution and expected outcome for each. This created a more structured basis for evaluating and prioritising potential solutions.

‍

Prioritise through shared evaluation.

I assessed hypotheses using a value-to-effort score and facilitated a MoSCoW workshop with stakeholders and developers. Priorities were challenged and agreed collaboratively.

‍

Challenge scope against user and business value.

A complete redesign of legacy apps would have taken approximately six months. I reduced the scope to targeted UI improvements, avoiding extra development effort where a lighter intervention was sufficient.

‍

Keep decisions traceable across teams.

I documented decisions and their rationale in Jira, creating a shared reference for design, development and stakeholders. Early Sales involvement helped connect research findings to customer needs. Feedback that staff physically unplugged the table, for example, reinforced the need to make shutdown easier to find.
